## 1. Tick and send rates of the major shooters, and what a higher rate buys

### Takeaway
Published rates span 20 Hz (Apex) to 128 Hz (Valorant); the arena shooters closest to the game under study sit at 60 Hz for small modes and 30 Hz for big-team modes (Halo Infinite). Developers agree the tick itself is worth only tens of ms (Respawn: 75 ms vs about 42 ms worst case going 20 to 60 Hz at 50 ms ping) and that consistent ticks, buffering and client frame rate matter as much.

### Cited Findings
- **Valorant (dev, 2020):** clients and servers both step movement on a fixed timestep of "exactly 128 times per second" regardless of render rate; target is 35 ms ping for 70% of players — [Riot tech blog, "Peeking into VALORANT's Netcode"](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode)
- **Valorant (dev, 2020-05-13):** "SERVER FRAMERATE = 7.8125ms (1/128th of a second)"; one-way lag target "<17.5ms for 70% of the player population" — [Riot, "04: On Peeker's Advantage & Ranked"](https://playvalorant.com/en-gb/news/game-updates/04-on-peeker-s-advantage-ranked/)
- **Apex Legends (dev, 2021-04-28):** servers run at 20 Hz (one world step per 50 ms); a full snapshot is saved and replicated each tick; the client input rate is not capped to the tick ("We process variable rate of inputs fine"), whereas "many games" cap input at the tick so a 120 fps client on a 60 Hz server loses half its inputs — [Respawn/EA, "Servers & Netcode Developer Deep Dive"](https://www.ea.com/games/apex-legends/news/servers-netcode-developer-deep-dive)
- **Apex, why not 60 Hz (dev, 2021):** at 50 ms ping and 60 fps, worst-case delay is 25 + 50 = 75 ms (about 5 frames) at 20 Hz versus 25 + 16.67 = 41.67 ms (about 3 frames) at 60 Hz; bandwidth would triple (about 60 kB/s to about 180 kB/s); Respawn says the gain "would feel like the difference between 58 FPS and 60 FPS" and "isn't enough to prioritize tickrate changes over other more efficient improvements". The post's own framing is inconsistent (it calls a worst-case calculation "best-case") — [Respawn/EA deep dive](https://www.ea.com/games/apex-legends/news/servers-netcode-developer-deep-dive)
- **Apex, overloaded servers (dev, 2021):** if a tick takes more than 50 ms "your game will slow down"; a bad server averaged about 200 ms per frame — [Respawn/EA deep dive](https://www.ea.com/games/apex-legends/news/servers-netcode-developer-deep-dive)
- **Halo Infinite (dev, early 2022):** "Our 4v4 matches run at a 60hz tick rate, and Big Team Battle games run at 30hz"; server processing adds 16 to 33 ms depending on mode, and a message arriving just after a tick starts waits that tick plus one more — [343 Industries, "Closer Look: Halo Infinite's Online Experience"](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience)
- **Halo 3 / MCC (snippet of press coverage of 343's April 2019 MCC update):** Halo 3 clients previously sent 15 packets/s and the server 30/s; the update raised Halo 2, 3, 4 and H2A to 60 Hz on dedicated and peer hosts — [GameRevolution](https://www.gamerevolution.com/news/519901-halo-mcc-april-update-increases-tick-rate); [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/halo-the-master-chief-collection-now-has-a-60-hz-tick-1833787526)
- **Halo 5 (dev, 2014 announcement):** 60 fps and dedicated servers only; no official server tick figure found — [Softpedia report of 343's announcement](https://games.softpedia.com/blog/Halo-5-Guardians-Runs-at-60fps-and-Has-Dedicated-Multiplayer-Servers-442638.shtml)
- **Overwatch (secondary summary of Tim Ford, GDC 2017):** fixed 16 ms "command frames" (about 62.5 Hz), 7 ms in tournament mode; the simulation rate is mandatory, so a slow machine runs two sim ticks per rendered frame and a server that cannot keep up enters "a death spiral" — [Edgegap deep dive on Ford's talk](https://edgegap.com/blog/game-backend-deep-dive-overwatch-2016-netcode-architecture-rollback); talk listing: [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024001/-Overwatch-Gameplay-Architecture-and)
- **Overwatch "high bandwidth" (press coverage of Blizzard's 2016 update):** PC clients went from about 21 updates/s (GamePro's headline says 20.8 Hz) to 60/s; players whose connection cannot keep up are dropped back to the old rate — [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/overwatch-netcode-tick-rate-60hz/); [GamePro headline](https://www.gamepro.de/artikel/overwatch-blizzard-erklaert-den-netcode-208hz-client-tickrate,3270422.html); [PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/overwatch/overwatch-netcode)
- **CS2 (secondary, article dated 2026-10-25):** still 64 ticks/s (15.6 ms) with "sub-tick" timestamps on shots, movement starts and grenade throws; game state remains tick-based. Valve's wording: sub-tick is "the heart of CS2's netcode", letting servers know "the exact instant that movement begins, a shot is fired, or a grenade is thrown" — [Team Dignitas guide](https://dignitas.gg/articles/how-cs2-processes-your-shots-a-guide-to-sub-tick-and-server-refresh-rates)
- **CS:GO (secondary table):** 64 tick on Valve matchmaking, 128 on third-party/pro servers — [DiamondLobby tick-rate table (unsourced, undated)](https://diamondlobby.com/server-tick-rates/)
- **Rocket League (community + Battle(non)sense measurement):** physics steps at 120 Hz on client and server; server sends about 60 updates/s — [Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/252950/discussions/0/1693788202025800838); [Game Developer on Battle(non)sense's test](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/console/how-i-rocket-league-i-s-netcode-stacks-up-against-other-online-games); primary talk (not opened): [Jared Cone, GDC 2018](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024972/)
- **Call of Duty (community measurements):** Modern Warfare (2019) standard modes averaged just over 60 Hz (Battle(non)sense via Dexerto); Warzone 2 measured about 20 to 24 Hz — [Dexerto](https://www.dexerto.com/call-of-duty/modern-warfare-network-analysis-troubling-inconsistencies-1289397); [Sportskeeda](https://www.sportskeeda.com/esports/warzone-2-s-terrible-server-performance-explained-tacticalbrit)
- **Battlefield 6 (snippet, headlines only, 2025):** 60 Hz at launch; the REDSEC battle royale runs 30 Hz and ramps to 60 Hz for the final fight — [Console & PC Gaming](https://consolepcgaming.com/battlefield-6-will-use-60hz-server-tick-rate-at-launch-visibility-fixes-coming-per-map); [same outlet on REDSEC](https://consolepcgaming.com/battlefield-redsec-runs-30hz-servers-that-ramp-to-60hz-for-the-final-fight-lead-producer-says)
- **The Finals (dev AMA, via Reddit mirror):** Embark attributes "being shot behind walls" to ping and tick rate, says raising the tick is not simple because the game is very CPU-heavy on the server, and that consistent ticks at the chosen rate matter more than a higher number; no official figure found (a Steam post claims 128, unverified) — [Embark AMA](https://lr.eu.psf.lt/r/thefinals/comments/1it1icj/ama_with_rob_gustav_matt_mikael_joakim_and_more); [Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2073850/discussions/0/4030225100434647282)
- **Fortnite (conflicting, third party):** most tables list 30 Hz; one outlet says Epic moved all servers to 60 Hz, with no source — [DiamondLobby](https://diamondlobby.com/server-tick-rates/); contradicted by [Gamereactor](https://www.gamereactor.eu/the-state-of-call-of-duty-warzone-20-is-shameful-1457383/)

### Inferences
- Average cost of the tick alone (wait half a tick, then one tick of processing): 20 Hz about 75 ms, 30 Hz about 50 ms, 60 Hz about 25 ms, 128 Hz about 11.7 ms. Going 30 to 60 Hz saves about 25 ms on every server-mediated event; 60 to 128 Hz saves about 13 ms. That is the same order as one rendered frame at 60 fps, which is why Respawn calls it small and why Riot pairs 128 Hz with low ping and high client fps.
- For a Halo-style game the published precedent is exactly "60 Hz small modes, 30 Hz big team" (Halo Infinite). A long time-to-kill hides single-tick errors in damage better than a one-shot game does, but it does not hide them in movement, melee and "who saw whom first".
- Embark's and Respawn's point generalises: a tick rate that the server misses under load (slow-motion, late snapshots) feels worse than a lower rate held steadily.

### Gaps
- Halo 2's original rate, Halo 5's server tick, Quake 3 / Quake Live snapshot rates and The Finals' real rate: no primary source found in this pass.
- Fortnite's current rate: sources conflict; no Epic statement found.
- Apex after 2021: I found no later official change to 20 Hz (an EA forum thread from 2026 asks the same question; not opened).
- Battle(non)sense's own delay tables were not retrievable as text.

## 2. Interpolation delay for remote players, and peeker's advantage

### Takeaway
Remote players are always drawn in the past; the published range runs from 7.8 ms (Valorant, one 128 Hz frame) through about 20 ms (Overwatch after its 60 Hz update) to 100 ms (Source's old default). Riot's formula makes the cost explicit: every ms of interpolation delay, tick length, ping and enemy frame time is a ms in which the peeker sees the holder first, and Riot measured that players feel 10 ms of it.

### Cited Findings
- **Source engine default (dev, via snippet):** "Valve's games still default to a minimum interpolation delay ('lerp') of 100ms", chosen so that even if one snapshot is lost there are still two to interpolate between; the wiki advises `cl_interp 0` so lerp becomes exactly what the update rate needs — [Valve Developer Wiki, Source Multiplayer Networking](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Source_Multiplayer_Networking)
- **Source lerp formula (dev, via snippet):** `min( max( cl_interp, cl_interp_ratio / cl_updaterate ), 0.25 )`; servers bound it with `sv_client_min_interp_ratio` / `sv_client_max_interp_ratio` — [Valve Developer Wiki, TF2 Network Graph](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/TF2_Network_Graph)
- **Valorant (dev, 2020):** "NETWORK INTERP DELAY = 7.8125ms" by default; the "Network Buffering" setting raises it: Minimum = 1 frame (7.8125 ms), Moderate = 3 frames (23.4375 ms, and the client's moves go up at 64 Hz), Maximum = 5 frames (39.0625 ms, moves at 32 Hz); Moderate gives opponents about 15.6 ms extra advantage, Maximum about 31.3 ms; "if you have stable internet, do not move off the 'Minimum' setting" — [Riot, 04 post](https://playvalorant.com/en-gb/news/game-updates/04-on-peeker-s-advantage-ranked/); [Riot developer post (mirror)](https://devtrackers.gg/valorant/p/e1133afd-peekers-advantage-strafe-shooting)
- **Valorant side effect (dev, 2020):** the interp delay causes "a 7.8125ms desync between movement data and damage data", which is why a strafing enemy can appear to shoot accurately while still moving; Riot considered a zero-interp option but it would show "player pops" under packet loss — [Riot, 04 post](https://playvalorant.com/en-gb/news/game-updates/04-on-peeker-s-advantage-ranked/)
- **Riot's peeker's-advantage formula (dev, 2020-05-13):** advantage = enemy client frame time + enemy one-way lag + server frame time + your one-way lag + network interp delay; "an average of 40-70ms" in Valorant; average human reaction time cited as about 247 ms — [Riot, 04 post](https://playvalorant.com/en-gb/news/game-updates/04-on-peeker-s-advantage-ranked/)
- **Riot's fuller buffering model (dev, 2020 tech blog):** server about 2 frames (0.5 waiting to enter the queue + 0.5 network buffer target + 1 to apply and send); client about 3 frames (0.5 wait + 1 full frame network buffer + 1 apply + about 0.5 GPU/swap-chain, which "can range from 0 to 2 frames"). Baseline: peekers get "~141ms longer to react than defenders"; Riot Direct routing plus 128-tick servers "shave off ~40ms (28%)"; a 144 fps client brings it to "~71ms (a 49% reduction)" — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode)
- **How much players feel (dev, 2020):** in Riot's playtests top-level fights were often decided by 20 to 50 ms, players detected changes of about 10 ms, and a 10 ms shift moved a matchup from roughly 90% for the angle-holder to 90% for the peeker — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode)
- **Riot's design counters to peeker's advantage (dev):** inaccurate fire while moving, time needed to stop, and hit tagging that slows a player hit by a non-lethal shot — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode)
- **Overwatch (snippet of 2016 press coverage):** interpolation delay fell from roughly 55 to 60 ms to about 20 ms with the 60 Hz update — [TweakTown](https://www.tweaktown.com/news/53864/overwatchs-new-high-bandwidth-update-rolls-pc/index.html); [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/overwatch-netcode-tick-rate-60hz/)
- **Glenn Fiedler (engineer's article):** an interpolation buffer should survive two lost packets in a row; with 2 to 5% loss that meant about 350 ms at 10 packets/s, about 150 ms at 30/s, about 85 ms at 60/s ("The solution is simple: increase the send rate!"); extrapolation "doesn't work very well" when motion is non-linear and unpredictable — [Gaffer On Games, Snapshot Interpolation](https://gafferongames.com/post/snapshot_interpolation/)
- **Halo Infinite, delay from trigger to the target's screen (dev, 2022):** shooter's latency to server + shooter's command buffer + server processing + target's latency from server; about 50 ms with both at 33 ms RTT, 83 ms with the shooter at 100 ms RTT, 108 ms at 150 ms RTT; a Spartan sprints at about 8.5 m/s and grapples at about 24 m/s, so small gaps become large position errors — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience)

### Inferences
- Riot's formula applied to other rates (assumptions: both players 35 ms RTT, peeker at 60 fps): 128 Hz with 1-tick interp = 16.7 + 17.5 + 7.8 + 17.5 + 7.8 = about 67 ms (matches Riot's 40 to 70 ms range). 60 Hz with 1-tick interp = about 85 ms; 60 Hz with 2-tick interp (the "survive one lost snapshot" rule) = about 102 ms. 30 Hz with 1-tick interp = about 118 ms; 30 Hz with 2-tick interp = about 152 ms. A 30 Hz big-team mode therefore gives a peeker roughly twice Valorant's head start before ping differences are counted.
- In distance, at Halo Infinite's 8.5 m/s sprint: 67 ms = 0.57 m, 102 ms = 0.87 m, 152 ms = 1.3 m of travel the holder never saw. A vehicle or grapple at 24 m/s covers 3.6 m in 152 ms.
- The two Riot figures (141/101/71 ms and 40 to 70 ms) come from two models: the tech blog adds every buffer on both machines; the dev post counts only the terms in its five-part sum. Quote them with their model, not as one number.
- Interpolation delay also hurts tracking: the target on screen is where it was `interp + latency` ago, so a strafing target's direction changes arrive late. With long time-to-kill, tracking aim is most of the fight, so this delay is felt continuously rather than only on the first shot.
- An adaptive buffer (Valorant's setting, Overwatch's fallback) is the standard compromise: short for clean connections, longer only when loss or jitter demands it. A fixed large buffer taxes everyone for the worst connection.

### Gaps
- Apex's and Halo Infinite's interpolation delays are not published in the sources read.
- Overwatch's exact interpolation numbers come from press summaries of a video I could not get a transcript of.
- No controlled study isolating the effect of remote interpolation delay on tracking accuracy was found; the claim above is an inference from the latency studies in section 6.

## 3. Lag compensation: models, caps, exceptions, hitscan vs projectile vs melee

### Takeaway
Every game studied rewinds targets to what the shooter saw ("favor the shooter") and accepts "shot behind cover" as the price; the differences are the cap (Source keeps up to 1 s of history, CS uses 0.2 s, Overwatch stops predicting hits above about 220 ms RTT) and the exceptions (TF2 does not compensate projectiles; Overwatch lets defensive abilities override the shooter; Halo Infinite gives melee a special path and still shipped with phasing and double back-smacks).

### Cited Findings
- **Source model (dev, via snippet):** lag compensation is the server using a player's latency to rewind time when processing a user command, to see what the player saw; `sv_maxunlag` is the number of seconds of player positions stored, default and maximum 1 — [Valve Developer Wiki, Lag Compensation](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Lag_Compensation)
- **CS:GO / CS2 cap (community reference):** `sv_maxunlag` defaults to 0.2 (200 ms) — [TotalCSGO command reference](https://totalcsgo.com/commands/svmaxunlag)
- **TF2 (community wiki of Valve behaviour):** hitscan and melee are lag-compensated; projectiles (rockets, grenades, syringes, flares), the flamethrower and airblast are not, "despite the effects appearing immediately after firing on a player's screen"; consequences include being "killed through a wall" by a Sniper and backstabs landing at the wrong angle ("facestabs") — [Team Fortress Wiki, Lag compensation](https://wiki.teamfortress.com/wiki/Lag_compensation)
- **The trade-off stated plainly (engineer's article):** "Because you shoot at him in the past, they may still be shot up to a few milliseconds after they took cover"; "It is somewhat unfair, but it's the most agreeable solution"; "It would be much worse to miss an unmissable shot!" — [Gabriel Gambetta, Lag Compensation](https://www.gabrielgambetta.com/lag-compensation.html)
- **Valorant (dev, 2020):** the server rewinds to the shooter's view; "it's also important to put some limits on how far the server is willing to rewind"; without a limit "a player with 500ms latency could kill you a half second after you'd moved behind cover". No numeric cap is published — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode)
- **Overwatch (secondary summary of Ford, GDC 2017):** the server rewinds to the shooter's frame; as a broad phase it tests the bullet against a volume enclosing about the last half-second of each target's movement and only fully rewinds those it touches; above about 220 ms RTT hit-impact prediction is switched off and the client extrapolates instead, "a deliberate fairness clamp" — [Edgegap on Ford's talk](https://edgegap.com/blog/game-backend-deep-dive-overwatch-2016-netcode-architecture-rollback)
- **Overwatch exceptions (press coverage of the 2016 developer video):** the netcode "basically always favours the shooter", except for special abilities; a patch made more abilities favour the defender when activated before the attacker's screen shows it. GamePro's summary (snippet): a survival ability such as Pharah's jump or Reaper's Wraith Form overrides the shooter's advantage — [PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/overwatch/cute-stickers-and-little-cups-help-explain-the-overwatch-netcode); [GamePro](https://www.gamepro.de/artikel/overwatch-blizzard-erklaert-den-netcode-208hz-client-tickrate,3270422.html)
- **Apex (dev, 2021):** the server is "a sort of time machine"; "Some games always give the advantage to players with lower ping, but we actively choose not to"; with a 50 ms and a 300 ms player the low-ping player wins simultaneous shots and corner peeks while each can be hit when apparently in cover, the mismatch being "distributed symmetrically". No rollback cap is stated — [Respawn/EA deep dive](https://www.ea.com/games/apex-legends/news/servers-netcode-developer-deep-dive)
- **Halo Infinite (dev, 2022):** "we choose to favor the shooter"; the shooter's client fires the projectile and shows tracers and impacts locally, the server "rewinds time and places all objects that they might be shooting towards", then sends the result; no rewind cap is stated; time is not rewound in Theater/observer, so shots there look like misses — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience)
- **Halo Infinite, shots after death (dev, 2022):** you may be dead on the server before your client knows; shots fired in that window are ignored, but "we do have a special code path that should allow the melee to go through even if you died on the server" — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience)
- **Halo Infinite, melee and phasing (dev, 2022):** during lunges two players' positions diverge between machines so each appears to pass through the other; each client can see a back-smack and the server applies both; permanent desync happens when the client fails to detect or correct a position disagreement — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience)
- **Halo Infinite, what happened next (press):** in 2022 343 said the developers who would fix desync "have been allocated to other Infinite work"; in Nov 2023 it said networking was being "fundamentally changed"; the new networking model went into Firefight playlists in Dec 2023 and the rest of multiplayer on 2024-03-19, aimed at desync, rubber-banding and damage around corners — [PC Gamer, 2022](https://pcgamer.com/halo-infinites-desync-problem-is-on-hold-because-developers-are-busy-with-other-things); [Pure Xbox, 2023](https://www.purexbox.com/news/2023/11/halo-infinites-networking-is-being-fundamentally-changed-to-address-de-sync-issues); [The FPS Review, 2024](https://www.thefpsreview.com/2024/03/19/halo-infinite-adds-overhauled-network-model-and-easy-anti-cheat-on-pc/)
- **Halo: Reach (secondary summary of David Aldridge, GDC 2011, "I Shot You First"):** host-authoritative with client requests; clients send target-relative shots ("I shot directly at the head of this player") and the host checks a clean line from gun to that point; muzzle flash and sound are immediate, damage waits for the host; grenade throw animation is predicted on button press but the grenade itself waits for the host, and "players didn't notice that delay"; a 100 ms round trip is a tenth of a second that "optimization cannot remove", only hide; testers had a "lag" button and fixes iterated until lag reports stopped correlating with lost games — [Wolfire's session summary](https://www.wolfire.com/blog/2011/03/GDC-Session-Summary-Halo-networking)
- **Research direction:** a Hong Kong PolyU thesis notes games impose limits on lag compensation and proposes deciding eligibility per shot rather than per shooter ("advanced lag compensation") — [PolyU thesis record](https://theses.lib.polyu.edu.hk/handle/200/9783?mode=full)

### Inferences
- For the game under study (server picks the rewind depth; "the client never names its own lag"), the published Source rule still applies: the rewind must equal the shooter's latency plus the interpolation delay the shooter's client was actually using when it drew the target. If the server's estimate of that view time is off by one tick, shots that looked dead-on miss by one tick of target travel (0.14 m at 60 Hz, 0.28 m at 30 Hz, at 8.5 m/s). This is the classic cause of "my shots don't register" in an otherwise correct design, and it is worst against strafing targets.
- A cap of about 200 to 250 ms is the common practice (CS 200 ms; Overwatch about 220 ms RTT). Beyond the cap the shooter must lead targets; the victim's worst-case "died behind cover" window is bounded by the cap plus their own latency and interp.
- Halo-specific: melee and projectiles need their own rules. TF2 does not rewind for projectiles; Halo Infinite's projectile is client-fired and server-resolved with rewind; lunging melee moves two bodies at once and produced phasing and double kills until the 2024 overhaul. Vehicles and grapple-speed movement (24 m/s) multiply every timing error by about 3 compared with sprinting.
- With recharging shields, the "died behind cover" complaint has a second cost: late damage arriving after the player reached cover also resets or delays the shield recharge they thought they had started.

### Gaps
- Yahn Bernier's 2001 paper and the Valve wiki pages could not be opened (HTTP 403); the exact "command execution time = server time − packet latency − client view interpolation" formula is confirmed only by a secondhand quote in a Steam discussion.
- Overwatch's often-quoted "no favor-the-shooter above 250 ms ping" could not be verified; the only figure I found is Edgegap's "about 220 ms RTT".
- Valorant's, Apex's and Halo Infinite's rewind caps are not published in the sources read.
- CS2's current lag-compensation internals: no Valve document found.

## 4. Client prediction, reconciliation, and predicting hit effects

### Takeaway
The standard is to predict the local player's movement, abilities and weapon effects, roll back and replay on disagreement, and keep damage server-authoritative. Predicting hit confirmation (blood, hit markers) trades responsiveness for occasional lies: Respawn defines "no reg" as exactly that mismatch, and Valve shipped it in CS2 only as an opt-in that turns itself off at high ping and that most pros leave off.

### Cited Findings
- **Overwatch prediction and rollback (secondary summary of Ford, GDC 2017):** movement, all abilities and weapons are predicted by default (teams must opt out); on a misprediction the client rolls back to the server's movement snapshot and replays buffered inputs; this depends on "highly deterministic" movement and a dedicated server; "Predicting rockets is rad", though rockets occasionally vanish — [Edgegap on Ford's talk](https://edgegap.com/blog/game-backend-deep-dive-overwatch-2016-netcode-architecture-rollback)
- **Overwatch clock and input redundancy (secondary):** the client runs ahead of the server by half the RTT plus one buffered command frame (about 96 ms at 160 ms RTT); every input packet carries all inputs since the last acknowledged state (a sliding window Ford traces to QuakeWorld); when the server starves for input it tells the client to dilate time (steps of about 15.2 ms instead of 16 ms) to build a buffer, and to drain it later — [Edgegap on Ford's talk](https://edgegap.com/blog/game-backend-deep-dive-overwatch-2016-netcode-architecture-rollback)
- **Valorant (dev, 2020):** when a client's input is missing the server predicts it by repeating the last input; clients simulate at 128 Hz and interpolate within a move update to render at frame boundaries — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode)
- **Halo Infinite (dev, 2022):** the client normally corrects to the server's position once a disagreement is detected; registration fails because of non-determinism between client and server (for example an unpredictable Warthog hit), bandwidth limits (the server prioritises players you shoot, players shooting you, and yourself) and dying on the server before the client knows; the PC "Simulation Quality: Ultra" setting gives more time to correcting determinism errors — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience)
- **Apex "no reg" (dev, 2021):** defined as the player seeing hit feedback (blood spray, sound) with no damage because the server disagrees; causes are latency and packet loss making the local simulation drift, and past ballistics bugs for bullets with non-point size — [Respawn/EA deep dive](https://www.ea.com/games/apex-legends/news/servers-netcode-developer-deep-dive)
- **CS2 damage prediction (press coverage of Valve's 2024-11-13/14 update):** lets clients "immediately play the audio/visual effects of inflicting damage without waiting for confirmation from the server"; three settings, two off by default; automatically disabled at high ping; a known failure is seeing a headshot effect, switching target, and the first enemy being alive; of 120 pro configs, 73 had everything off and 41 only kill ragdolls on — [Sportskeeda patch notes](https://www.sportskeeda.com/esports/cs2-patch-notes-november-14-2024-train-added-damage-prediction-overpass-updates); [BLAST.tv](https://blast.tv/cs/news/damage-prediction-in-cs2); [bo3.gg](https://bo3.gg/articles/what-is-damage-prediction-in-cs2)
- **Valorant hit-registration clarity (dev, 2020):** Riot separates "correctness" bugs from "clarity" bugs (a shoulder hit that looks like a headshot); it moved blood and spark effects to spawn on the character's current position and follow it, because players running into stale hit effects misread where shots landed (Patch 1.07) — [Riot, "The State of Hit Registration"](https://playvalorant.com/en-us/news/dev/the-state-of-hit-registration/)
- **Halo: Reach (secondary):** muzzle flash and gunshot sound are immediate; damage waits for the host — [Wolfire's summary of Aldridge](https://www.wolfire.com/blog/2011/03/GDC-Session-Summary-Halo-networking)
- **Halo Infinite (dev, 2022):** tracers and impacts are shown locally at once; hit markers appear when the server's result arrives — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience)

### Inferences
- A consistent pattern across Halo, Valorant and Overwatch: fire effects (muzzle, tracer, recoil, sound) are predicted; damage confirmation (hit marker, shield flare on the victim, kill) waits for the server. The cost is that confirmation lags by RTT + server buffer + one tick, which at 30 Hz and 60 ms ping is about 110 to 130 ms; with shields and long time-to-kill the player gets many confirmations per kill, so a late hit marker reads as "mushy" guns rather than as a single missed shot.
- If the game under study predicts any hit feedback, the CS2 precedent suggests gating it by ping and never predicting kills.
- Rubber-banding has three published sources: non-determinism (Halo Infinite), the server running out of the client's input (Overwatch's time dilation exists to prevent it), and server overload (Apex's slow-motion servers). A deterministic sim shared by client and server removes the first only if the client's inputs to it (view angles, tick alignment, map state) are bit-identical.

### Gaps
- No primary source was retrieved for CS:GO's client-side "blood but no damage" behaviour; the CS2 setting is the best documented analogue.
- Gambetta's prediction/reconciliation pages and Quake 3/QuakeWorld primary material were not fetched in this pass; only his lag-compensation page was read.
- How mispredictions are visually smoothed (Source's `cl_smooth`) could not be verified because the Valve wiki was unreachable.

## 5. Input handling: per-frame aim, tick quantisation, sub-tick, server input buffers

### Takeaway
The documented practice is: rotate the camera from mouse input every rendered frame, simulate movement on the fixed tick, interpolate only translation for display, and timestamp or carry the exact aim with each shot. Quantising aim or the camera to a 30 or 60 Hz tick, or drawing the camera one tick late, adds 17 to 33 ms of local latency, which the research in section 6 says is about twice as harmful as the same amount of network latency.

### Cited Findings
- **Bevy's official guidance (engine example, current main):** fixed-timestep rendering interpolation "is a tradeoff: every visual frame is now slightly lagging behind the actual physical frame"; "Cameras should update as often as possible, or the player will very quickly notice choppy movement if it's only updated at the same rate as the physics simulation"; rotate the camera every frame before the fixed loop (using `AccumulatedMouseMotion`), accumulate input for the fixed step, and set the camera's translation from the interpolated position after the fixed loop — [bevy/examples/movement/physics_in_fixed_timestep.rs](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/blob/main/examples/movement/physics_in_fixed_timestep.rs)
- **Fix Your Timestep (engineer's article):** the renderer "produces time and the simulation consumes it in discrete dt sized steps"; the leftover accumulator gives a blend factor between the previous and current state to remove stutter — [Gaffer On Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/fix_your_timestep/)
- **CS2 sub-tick (secondary, 2026) and its reception (press, 2023 to 2025):** the client timestamps actions between ticks and the server evaluates them at that instant; players reported laggy-feeling movement and being shot behind cover and many argue plain 128 tick would be better; a 2025 survey of pros named sub-tick as a top complaint; a secondhand report says Valve itself is unhappy with it and that 128 tick is "on the table"; a third-party page says Valve shipped a "TrueView" update in Nov 2025 to address remaining visual lag — [Dignitas](https://dignitas.gg/articles/how-cs2-processes-your-shots-a-guide-to-sub-tick-and-server-refresh-rates); [Sportskeeda](https://sportskeeda.com/esports/even-64-tick-better-cs2-community-debates-sub-tick-system); [Dust2.us (secondhand)](https://www.dust2.us/news/75511/freakazoid-valens-visited-valves-office-theyre-aware-of-how-bad-subtick-is-right-now); [Profilerr on TrueView](https://profilerr.net/trueview-update-in-cs2/)
- **Apex (dev, 2021):** input is processed at the client's variable rate, not capped to the 20 Hz tick — [Respawn/EA deep dive](https://www.ea.com/games/apex-legends/news/servers-netcode-developer-deep-dive)
- **Server input (de-jitter) buffers (dev):** Halo Infinite's command buffer is 0 or 1 frame on a good connection (up to 16 ms extra) and several frames on a bad one, and it is a term in the shot-delay sum; Riot keeps the server queue "as short as possible" with a target of half a frame; Overwatch keeps one buffered command frame and grows it by time dilation under loss — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience); [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode); [Edgegap on Ford's talk](https://edgegap.com/blog/game-backend-deep-dive-overwatch-2016-netcode-architecture-rollback)
- **Client frame rate is part of the budget (dev, 2020):** Riot's model charges the client about 3 frames of buffering, so 60 fps costs about 50 ms and 144 fps about 21 ms; 144 fps alone took peeker's advantage from about 101 ms to about 71 ms — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode)

### Inferences
- Cost of tying aim to the tick: if view angles are applied only at tick boundaries, mouse-to-camera delay grows by half a tick on average and a full tick at worst (8/17 ms at 60 Hz, 17/33 ms at 30 Hz). If the first-person camera is additionally drawn from the interpolated (previous-to-current) state, add up to one more tick. At 30 Hz that is up to 66 ms of extra local latency before the display pipeline, which is in the range Nvidia and Claypool show measurably reduces accuracy. Symptom: "aim feels heavy/floaty", and a visible difference between 4v4 and big-team modes if they tick differently.
- Sub-tick's lesson: timestamping the action precisely is not enough if the visible result (animation, tracer, the player's own movement) still appears on the next tick. Players judged CS2 by what they saw and felt, not by server correctness.
- The shot must carry the aim the player actually had on the rendered frame they clicked (or a sub-tick timestamp), otherwise a flick shot is resolved with the previous tick's angles: at 30 Hz a 180 degree/s flick is 6 degrees off per tick.
- Every tick of server input buffer is a tick added to "my shots come out late" for everyone watching and to hit confirmation for the shooter. A browser tab that renders slowly sends input in bursts, which forces the server to hold a deeper buffer for that player; their opponents then see them later and they get their own confirmations later.

### Gaps
- No Valve primary text on sub-tick could be fetched (the counter-strike.net page returned only a title); claims about Valve's intentions are secondhand.
- I found no published measurement of the accuracy cost of tick-quantised view angles specifically; the numbers above combine tick arithmetic with the general latency studies.
- How Halo, Apex or Valorant sample mouse input relative to the tick is not described in the sources read.

## 6. End-to-end latency perception: what players notice and what it costs

### Takeaway
Local (hand-to-eye) latency hurts about twice as much as the same amount of network latency (Claypool's group: 100 ms less local latency = +6 points of accuracy vs +2 for network), 25 vs 85 ms of system latency roughly halves flick performance (Nvidia), people start to perceive mouse latency around 60 ms and trained players react to differences of about 10 ms in who-sees-whom-first (Riot).

### Cited Findings
- **Local vs network latency (research, 2021, 68 experienced CS:GO players, over 60 hours of play):** base local latency was 25 ms (high-speed camera); added local latency 0 to 100 ms, added network latency 25 to 150 ms. From a 125 ms total baseline, removing 100 ms of local latency improved accuracy by about 6 percentage points, score by 3 points/min and QoE by 1.6 on a 5-point scale; removing 100 ms of network latency improved them by about 2 points, 2 points/min and 0.7. "Local latency has about twice the impact"; regression slopes: accuracy −0.059 %/ms local vs −0.022 %/ms network. "Often less than a single point in a game separates the scores of top CS:GO players" — [Liu, Claypool, Kuwahara, Scovell, Sherman, "The Effects of Network Latency on CS:GO... Network vs. Local Latency on FPS Gamers", EHPHCI '21](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~claypool/papers/csgo-net-local-21/paper.pdf)
- **Network latency below 100 ms still matters (research, snippet):** a WPI CS:GO study reports degraded accuracy, score and QoE even under 100 ms, up to about 15% from 0 to 100 ms — [WPI technical report 21-08](https://ftp.cs.wpi.edu/pub/techreports/pdf/21-08.pdf)
- **Latency vs refresh rate (research, SIGGRAPH Asia 2019, 8 skilled esports players):** "reduced latency has a clear benefit in task completion time while increased refresh rate has relatively minor effects on performance when the inherent latency reduction present at high refresh rates is removed"; a small, marginally significant benefit of refresh rate alone for some tracking tasks — [Spjut et al., Nvidia Research](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/amri/publication/spjut2019latency)
- **Nvidia/KovaaK's system-latency experiment (vendor research, Dec 2021 to Apr 2022, over 12,000 players):** at 25 / 55 / 85 ms of system latency, mean targets hit were 112.6 / 103.8 / 90.6 in a multi-target task (about 24% better at 25 than 85 ms) and 15.2 / 11.2 / 7.7 in a 600 ms flick task (about 2x); top-10% medians 31 / 22 / 15; mouse and monitor latency were only estimated — [Nvidia developer blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/improving-player-performance-with-low-latency-as-evident-from-fps-aim-trainer-experiments/)
- **A reviewer's perception of those steps:** 25 vs 85 ms was easy to tell apart, 55 was hard to tell from 25 — [Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/computing/nvidia-system-latency-challenge-results-shocked-me)
- **Perception thresholds for mouse interaction (research, 2017):** thresholds for a simple mouse task "lie in the range of 60 ms" and vary with the person; earlier stylus work found 30 to 80 ms and 60 to 105 ms. A secondary citation gives 34 to 137 ms (mean 65 ms) for cursor dragging — [Forch, Franke, Rauh, Krems, "Are 100 ms Fast Enough?"](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/are-100-ms-fast-enough-characterizing-latency-perception-threshol/)
- **Competitive players are far more sensitive in duels (dev, 2020):** Riot's playtesters detected about 10 ms changes in peeker's advantage; fights were decided by 20 to 50 ms — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode)
- **Published budgets (dev):** Riot: 128 Hz, 35 ms RTT for 70% of players (17.5 ms one way), 7.8 ms interp, about 2 server frames and 3 client frames of buffering; Halo Infinite: about 50 ms from trigger to the target's screen when both players are at 33 ms RTT on 60 Hz — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode); [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience)
- **Rule of thumb from the web platform team:** "latencies longer than 50 milliseconds can interfere with a user's hand-eye coordination" — [Chrome developer blog on the desynchronized hint](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/desynchronized)

### Inferences
- Budget priority follows from the 2x finding: a ms removed between hand and eye on the player's own machine (tick-bound aim, vsync queues, browser compositor, mouse filtering) is worth about two ms removed from the network path. This matters most for the browser build, where the local path is the long one.
- A workable target implied by the studies: total click-to-photon near 25 ms is "good PC", 55 ms is measurably worse on flicks, 85 ms halves flick performance. Anything a game adds on top of the platform (tick quantisation, an extra interpolated frame) should be judged against the 30 ms step sizes these studies used.
- Sensitivity differs by task: flicking and "who shoots first" are the most latency-sensitive; multi-target tasks with planning less so. A long-TTK shooter is dominated by tracking, where Nvidia saw smaller but real effects and where remote interpolation delay adds to the error.

### Gaps
- No Nvidia document with per-title "esports latency budgets" was retrieved; only the 25/55/85 ms experimental conditions.
- Exact mean and variance of Forch et al.'s thresholds were not retrieved (abstract only).
- No peer-reviewed study of just-noticeable latency specifically for first-person mouse aim was found; touchscreen dragging JNDs of about 6 ms exist but come from patent documents and a different task.

## 7. Browser-specific latency and input issues

### Takeaway
As of Nov 2026 raw (unaccelerated) pointer-lock input exists in Chrome/Edge (not on Linux), Firefox 152+ and desktop Safari 18.4+, but it must be requested explicitly, and the Rust windowing layer Bevy uses (winit 0.30) does not request it. Browser frames are vsync-bound, mouse moves are batched to one delivery per frame, and the platform offers no control over presentation queue depth, so a browser build should be assumed to carry at least one to two frames more hand-to-eye latency than native, plus 10 to 40 ms of audio output latency on desktop.

### Cited Findings
- **Raw mouse input support (BCD, read 2026-11-09):** `requestPointerLock({unadjustedMovement: true})`: Chrome/Edge 88+ with the note "Supported on macOS Catalina 10.15.1+, Windows, and ChromeOS. Not yet supported on Linux"; Firefox 152+ (not Android); Safari 18.4+ (no pointer lock at all on iOS). The promise-returning form: Chrome 92, Safari 18.4 — [mdn/browser-compat-data, api/Element.json](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/Element.json)
- **Still not Baseline:** MDN marks `requestPointerLock()` as not Baseline; the option "will disable mouse acceleration"; when unsupported the request rejects with `NotSupportedError` and the page should retry without the option — [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Element/requestPointerLock); [web.dev (2020)](https://web.dev/articles/disable-mouse-acceleration)
- **Firefox shipped it in 2026 and hit a bug:** unadjusted movement was enabled by default in Firefox 152; on Windows in pointer lock, `pointerrawupdate` then delivered deltas without the cursor-recentre corrections, so accumulated movement was too fast, worse at 1000 Hz than at 125 Hz; fixed for Firefox 154 by reworking pointer lock — [Mozilla bug 2037802](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=2037802); [Mozilla bug 2055058](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=2055058)
- **Another 2026 Firefox report (snippet):** on macOS every `getCoalescedEvents()` entry of a `pointerrawupdate` event reported movement 0 while the event itself had the right delta — [Mozilla bug 2071024](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=2071024)
- **Mouse events are batched per frame:** since Chrome 60, continuous events (`mousemove`, `pointermove`) are delayed and dispatched once per frame just before the `requestAnimationFrame` callback; `getCoalescedEvents()` (Chrome 58) returns the individual samples; mice typically report at about 100 Hz and up to 2000 Hz — [Chrome developer blog, "Aligning input events"](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/aligning-input-events)
- **API availability (BCD):** `getCoalescedEvents`: Chrome 58, Firefox 59, Safari 18.2; `pointerrawupdate` (un-aligned raw events): Chrome 77 (secure contexts only since 142), Firefox 148, Safari none — [api/PointerEvent.json](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/PointerEvent.json); [api/Element.json](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/Element.json)
- **Pointer-lock delta spikes (community):** large random `movementX/Y` spikes in Chromium with high-polling mice (bad at 1000 Hz, gone at 250 Hz), reported still present in Chromium 94; a published workaround is to force `unadjustedMovement` — [Stack Overflow thread (mirror)](https://kiwix.lema.org/stackoverflow.com_en_all_2023-11/questions/47985847/javascript-mouseevent-movementx-and-movementy-large-spikes)
- **What Bevy's windowing layer does on the web (source, winit v0.30.x):** cursor lock calls `request_pointer_lock()` with no options, so OS acceleration stays on; mouse motion comes from `pointermove` expanded through `getCoalescedEvents()` when available, with a workaround comment "Firefox has wrong movement values in coalesced events" and a "TODO: Remove when Safari supports getCoalescedEvents"; redraws are driven by `requestAnimationFrame` — [winit canvas.rs](https://github.com/rust-windowing/winit/blob/v0.30.x/src/platform_impl/web/web_sys/canvas.rs); [winit event.rs](https://github.com/rust-windowing/winit/blob/v0.30.x/src/platform_impl/web/web_sys/event.rs)
- **Presentation latency cannot be tuned from a page (engineer's article, 2022):** "WebGL and requestAnimationFrame give you no explicit control over the queue depth"; the author measured no gain on Windows from `desynchronized: true`, `pointerrawupdate` or OffscreenCanvas; without queue-depth control, input latency of WebGL and WebGPU content "is going to remain poor". For comparison, native Windows: the desktop compositor adds one frame, a DXGI swap chain with a latency limit about two — [James Darpinian, "Input Latency: Platform-specific Considerations"](https://james-darpinian-com.pages.dev/blog/latency-platform-considerations)
- **The `desynchronized` canvas hint:** bypasses the page compositor and in some cases sends the canvas buffer straight to the display controller; may tear; WebGL needs `preserveDrawingBuffer: true`. Support (BCD): WebGL/WebGL2 in Chrome 81+ on ChromeOS and Windows only; not Firefox, not Safari — [Chrome developer blog](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/desynchronized); [api/HTMLCanvasElement.json](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/HTMLCanvasElement.json)
- **Vsync is effectively forced on the web (engine docs):** Bevy's `AutoNoVsync` resolves to "Immediate -> Mailbox -> Fifo (on web)" — [bevy_window/src/window.rs, v0.18.0](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/blob/v0.18.0/crates/bevy_window/src/window.rs)
- **WebGPU availability (BCD):** canvas `webgpu` context in Chrome (ChromeOS, macOS, Windows, and Linux on Intel Gen12+ only), Firefox 141+ on Windows only, Safari 26+ — [api/HTMLCanvasElement.json](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/HTMLCanvasElement.json)
- **Audio latency reporting (BCD):** `AudioContext.baseLatency`: Chrome 58, Firefox 70, Safari 14.1; `outputLatency`: Chrome 102, Firefox 70, Safari 18.4 (MDN: Baseline 2025, newly available); the `latencyHint` constructor option is not supported in Firefox; `renderSizeHint` is Chrome 153+ only — [api/AudioContext.json](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/AudioContext.json); [MDN outputLatency](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/API/AudioContext/outputLatency)
- **Typical audio output latency by platform (browser audio engineer's notes, undated, older):** "on the order of 10ms on Windows (post-Vista, using WASAPI)", "a few milliseconds" on macOS/iOS, "30-40ms on Linux/PulseAudio", Android from 12.5 ms to "up to 150ms on some very cheap devices" — [Paul Adenot, Web Audio API performance and debugging notes](https://padenot.github.io/web-audio-perf/)
- **Gamepads are polled:** the Gamepad API is polled with `navigator.getGamepads()`, usually once per animation frame (about 60 Hz) while the OS reads the pad at up to 250 Hz, so inputs can be missed; an event-driven API (`rawgamepadinputchange`) was at "Intent to Prototype" in Chromium and reported in May 2025 as being built by Microsoft; one WebKit bug measured Safari at about 108 ms vs Chrome 56 ms for a wired pad — [Edge explainer](https://microsoftedge.github.io/MSEdgeExplainers/GamepadEventDrivenInputAPI/explainer.html); [blink-dev Intent to Prototype](https://groups.google.com/a/chromium.org/g/blink-dev/c/5IpJBqhqrMg); [Windows Latest, 2025-05](https://windowslatest.com/2025/05/27/microsoft-wants-to-make-gamepad-gaming-faster-on-chrome-for-windows-11); [WebKit bug 217742](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=217742)

### Inferences
- Expected extra hand-to-eye latency of a browser shooter over the same game native, built from the cited mechanisms: mouse samples wait for the next frame (0 to 1 frame, average half), the frame is vsync-bound with no tearing option (native with vsync off has none of this), and the browser's compositor plus the OS compositor queue add about 1 to 2 frames. At 60 Hz that is roughly 25 to 50 ms more than a native build running uncapped, and about 10 to 20 ms more at 144 Hz. I found no direct published measurement; this should be measured (high-speed camera or photodiode) rather than quoted.
- If the page obtains pointer lock through winit/Bevy rather than its own JavaScript, the browser build has OS mouse acceleration on while the desktop build has raw input: the same player gets different aim on the two builds, and their sensitivity is not transferable. This is cheap to check and fix (request the lock with `unadjustedMovement: true`, fall back on `NotSupportedError`) and is the single most "feel"-relevant browser API in this list. Chrome on Linux cannot get raw input at all.
- Input-to-sound: Web Audio adds its render quantum plus the device latency. Using Adenot's figures, expect about 10 to 15 ms on Windows, under 10 ms on macOS, 30 to 40 ms or more on Linux, and up to 150 ms on poor Android devices; Bluetooth headphones add far more. A gunshot or hit sound that is scheduled from the main thread also inherits frame-time jitter; `outputLatency` lets the game measure and report it but not remove it.
- Single-threaded wasm means any main-thread stall (shader compilation on first use, asset decode, GC of JS-side objects) stops input sampling, simulation and sending at once; to the server this looks like a burst of late inputs, and to the player as a hitch followed by a correction.
- Gamepad aim in the browser is quantised to the frame rate and, on Safari, may be far later; treat pad latency as a separate budget from mouse.

### Gaps
- No engineering write-ups from Krunker, Venge or Shell Shockers on netcode or input latency were found (search returned only SEO pages and fan guides).
- No published, controlled click-to-photon comparison of the same game in Chrome/Firefox/Safari versus native was found; no published WebGL2-versus-WebGPU presentation latency comparison was found.
- Chrome-on-Linux raw input: BCD still says unsupported; I did not find a Chromium bug with a ship date.
- Whether Chrome has shipped event-driven gamepad input by Nov 2026 is unconfirmed.
- Current per-browser `outputLatency` values in 2026 were not found; Adenot's figures are several years old.

## 8. Transport: WebTransport vs WebRTC data channels vs WebSocket

### Takeaway
WebTransport (unreliable datagrams over HTTP/3) became available in all three engines when Safari 26.4 shipped in March 2026, which removes the main reason to fall back to WebSocket; the remaining pitfalls are API differences between engines for sending datagrams and choosing congestion control, and QUIC congestion control that is not tuned for real-time traffic.

### Cited Findings
- **Support (BCD, read 2026-11-09):** `WebTransport`: Chrome/Edge 97, Firefox 114, Safari 26.4 (desktop and iOS); `datagrams`: same versions — [api/WebTransport.json](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/WebTransport.json)
- **Baseline status:** MDN marks `WebTransport.datagrams` "Baseline 2026, newly available" since March 2026 — [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/API/WebTransport/datagrams)
- **Engine differences that matter for a game (BCD):** `datagrams.writable` exists in Chrome and Firefox but not Safari; the newer `datagrams.createWritable()` exists in Safari 26.4 and Firefox 155 but not Chrome; the `congestionControl` constructor option (default / throughput / low-latency) exists in Firefox 114 and Safari 26.4 but not Chrome; `serverCertificateHashes` (self-signed dev/edge certificates): Chrome 100, Firefox 125, Safari 26.4; `outgoingMaxAge` / `incomingMaxAge` (drop stale datagrams): Chrome 97, Firefox 114, Safari 26.4 — [api/WebTransportDatagramDuplexStream.json](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/WebTransportDatagramDuplexStream.json); [api/WebTransport.json](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/WebTransport.json)
- **Interop work continues (blog, 2026):** WebTransport is a focus area of Interop 2026; engines still differ on server requirements and certificate handling — [Utsubo, "Frontier Web APIs in 2026"](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/frontier-web-apis-2026-production-ready)
- **Head-of-line blocking:** WebSocket runs on TCP, so one lost packet stalls everything behind it; QUIC streams stall independently and datagrams are never retransmitted, which suits "latest value wins" state such as positions — [OpenReplay blog](https://blog.openreplay.com/low-latency-browser-communication-webtransport/)
- **Congestion control is a known weak point (mailing list):** a web-transport-dev post argues WebTransport with BBRv1/v2 "is not suitable for applications requiring low or ultra-low latency", for datagrams and streams alike; a 2022 thread reported Chrome's implementation "does not work very well in realtime communications" — [web-transport-dev thread](https://groups.google.com/a/chromium.org/g/web-transport-dev/c/dWusOjQa9f0)
- **WebRTC data channels:** SCTP over DTLS over UDP, designed peer-to-peer, need ICE/STUN/TURN and a signalling channel even for client-server use — [OpenReplay blog](https://blog.openreplay.com/low-latency-browser-communication-webtransport/)

### Inferences
- How transport problems read to a player: on a TCP/WebSocket path a single lost packet freezes all remote players for about one retransmission timeout (often 200 ms or more) and then they fast-forward ("teleporty"), and the player's own inputs arrive at the server in a clump (their shots land late, then all at once). On QUIC datagrams the same loss costs one snapshot, which a 2-snapshot interpolation buffer hides.
- QUIC's congestion controller can still queue or pace datagrams when it believes the path is congested; with a fixed-rate game stream this shows up as periodic delay growth rather than loss. Setting a short `outgoingMaxAge` (about one or two ticks) makes the browser drop stale input instead of delivering it late.
- Any reliable stream used for gameplay-critical events (kills, spawns, weapon pickups) reintroduces head-of-line blocking for those events only; if hit confirmation rides a reliable stream while positions ride datagrams, confirmation can lag behind what the player sees after a loss.
- With Safari on board, a WebSocket fallback is now needed only for networks that block UDP/443; players on such networks will have categorically worse feel and are worth labelling in telemetry.

### Gaps
- No measured data on gameplay hitches under loss for the three transports was found; the above is mechanism-level.
- Which congestion controller each browser uses for WebTransport datagrams in 2026 is not confirmed (one Mozilla bug comment maps "low-latency" to BBRv1 and "throughput" to BBRv2; unverified).
- Safari 26.4's real-world datagram behaviour (loss, pacing) is too new for published field reports that I could find.

## 9. Server-side visibility culling (fog of war) as anti-wallhack, and its pop-in problem

### Takeaway
Riot's Valorant system is the documented precedent for exactly this design: withhold an enemy until just before they could be seen, and avoid pop-in by expanding the visibility test with velocity times a look-ahead "bigger than the expected ping time", erring towards revealing early. Riot published the method and the bugs it caused, but not the look-ahead value.

### Cited Findings
- **Design (dev, 2020):** the server withholds enemy positions until the client needs them ("If an opponent was behind a wall, we wouldn't send their location"); built on Unreal's network relevancy with eventually consistent replication; modelled on League of Legends' fog of war — [Riot tech blog, "Demolishing Wallhacks with VALORANT's Fog of War"](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/demolishing-wallhacks-valorants-fog-war)
- **Pop-in and how it was fixed (dev, 2020):** the first prototype (one ray to the actor's centre each tick) caused pop-in and actors that stayed invisible; ten rays to bounding-box corners were still fallible and about 10x the cost; the fix was to expand the bounding box by velocity times a look-ahead time "bigger than the expected ping time" so data is sent just before an enemy rounds a corner, combined with a precomputed potentially-visible-set between map cells that returns visible if any part of one cell can see any part of the other (optimistic by design) — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/demolishing-wallhacks-valorants-fog-war)
- **Hysteresis and cost (dev, 2020):** actors once relevant stay relevant briefly; attached actors (weapons) inherit their parent's visibility; cost fell from 50% of the server frame (which had dropped the server from 128 to 64 tick) to under 2%, and fog of war is a net performance gain because less is sent — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/demolishing-wallhacks-valorants-fog-war)
- **Secondary bugs from hiding actors (dev, 2020):** one-shot events sent while an actor was hidden were lost (for example a player emerging in the wrong pose after "Start Spike Defuse"); Riot built "Effect Containers" that restart and fast-forward effects with their remaining duration when an actor leaves the fog; sound needed separate integration — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/demolishing-wallhacks-valorants-fog-war)
- **How Riot tunes it (dev, 2020):** bots enter and leave fog on every build; telemetry on every query with dashboards of time enemies are relevant and why, per map and agent, with alerts on outliers; future tuning from latency and cheat data — [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/demolishing-wallhacks-valorants-fog-war)
- **Speeds that set the margin in a Halo-style game (dev, 2022):** sprint about 8.5 m/s, grapple about 24 m/s — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience)

### Inferences
- Minimum look-ahead for no pop-in: the reveal must reach the peeking client and pass through its interpolation buffer before line of sight opens. That needs about (viewer's RTT + viewer's interp delay + one server tick + snapshot jitter margin), and the expansion must use the closing speed of both players, because the viewer's own predicted position is ahead of the server's by about half their RTT plus their input buffer. Example: 100 ms RTT, 33 ms interp, 33 ms tick, 33 ms margin = about 200 ms; at 8.5 m/s each that is 1.7 m of expansion per player, 3.4 m combined; a 24 m/s mover needs 4.8 m on its own.
- If the margin is sized for typical ping and not per-client, high-ping players get pop-in exactly when they peek: the enemy appears a few frames after the corner clears. To the player this is "they saw me first" and is indistinguishable from peeker's advantage, but unlike peeker's advantage it is fixable by widening the margin for that client.
- A just-revealed enemy has no interpolation history on the client. Unless the first snapshots are backfilled or the entity is revealed a full buffer early, it will either appear late (waiting to fill the buffer) or snap/slide for its first few frames ("enemies teleport when they come round corners").
- State carried by events (reload in progress, shield state, crouch, weapon swap, vehicle seat) must be re-sent as state on reveal, per Riot's Effect Containers lesson; otherwise the first frames of a newly visible enemy show the wrong pose or weapon, which also misleads the player about threat.
- Fast vehicles and long sightlines in an 8v8 terrain map are the hard case for this design: open terrain makes most pairs potentially visible anyway (little anti-cheat value) while high speeds demand the largest margins.

### Gaps
- Riot never published the look-ahead time or distance; the numbers above are my arithmetic.
- No primary source on CS:GO/CS2's server-side occlusion anti-wallhack or its pop-in behaviour was found; searches returned only wallhack-exploit news and a third-party plugin.
- League of Legends' network-side fog of war: not researched beyond Riot's remark that Valorant's was modelled on it.

## 10. Bevy-engine specifics (Bevy 0.18, winit 0.30, wgpu 27 as used by this client)

### Takeaway
Bevy's defaults favour throughput over latency: pipelined rendering renders frame N while frame N+1 simulates, the swap chain keeps up to 2 queued frames by default under a vsync FIFO "approximately 3 frames long", and input is read at the start of the frame. The documented low-latency levers are the present mode, `desired_maximum_frame_latency = 1`, frame pacing (`bevy_framepace`), and rotating the camera per frame outside the fixed step; on the web none of the presentation levers apply.

### Cited Findings
- **Pipelined rendering (engine docs):** "moves rendering into a different thread, so that the Nth frame's rendering can be run at the same time as the N + 1 frame's simulation"; within a frame the order is sync, extract, then winit events, then the main schedule — [bevy_render/src/pipelined_rendering.rs](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/blob/main/crates/bevy_render/src/pipelined_rendering.rs)
- **Swap-chain queue depth (engine docs, 0.18):** `Window::desired_maximum_frame_latency` is the "maximum number of queued frames admissible on the GPU. Given values are usually within the 1-3 range. If not provided, this will default to 2" — [bevy_window/src/window.rs, v0.18.0](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/blob/v0.18.0/crates/bevy_window/src/window.rs)
- **Why it was exposed (engine issue, merged 2024-04-19):** the proposal was to use 1 when pipelined rendering is disabled for lower latency; maintainers kept 2 as default because it may avoid CPU stalls when a GPU frame runs long, and asked for latency benchmarks — [Bevy issue #12930](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/issues/12930)
- **Present modes (engine docs, 0.18):** `AutoVsync` = FifoRelaxed then Fifo; `AutoNoVsync` = Immediate, then Mailbox, then Fifo on web; Fifo keeps "a First-In-First-Out queue approximately 3 frames long" — [bevy_window/src/window.rs, v0.18.0](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/blob/v0.18.0/crates/bevy_window/src/window.rs)
- **Frame pacing (community plugin):** `bevy_framepace` sleeps the main thread so "the next frame isn't started until the very last moment", which "reduces motion-to-photon latency by moving reading input closer to rendering the frame"; version 0.21 supports Bevy 0.18 (its table lists support up to Bevy 0.20) — [bevy_framepace README](https://github.com/aevyrie/bevy_framepace)
- **Reported input-latency issues (community):** input is polled right after the previous frame ends, giving about one frame of latency; a Windows/DX12 user removed a felt delay by switching to `PresentMode::Immediate`; stutter with high-polling-rate mice traced towards winit (winit issues #1418, PR #3609) — [Bevypunk issue #2](https://www.github.com/IDEDARY/Bevypunk/issues/2); [Bevy discussion #16335](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/discussions/16335); [Bevy issue #12679](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/issues/12679)
- **Fixed timestep and cameras (official example):** see section 5: rotate the camera each frame in `RunFixedMainLoopSystems::BeforeFixedMainLoop`, simulate in `FixedUpdate`, interpolate translation in `AfterFixedMainLoop`; the example states interpolation makes "every visual frame ... slightly lagging behind the actual physical frame" — [physics_in_fixed_timestep.rs](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/blob/main/examples/movement/physics_in_fixed_timestep.rs)
- **Web input path (winit 0.30 source):** pointer lock without `unadjustedMovement`, `pointermove` + coalesced events, no `pointerrawupdate`, redraw on `requestAnimationFrame` (details in section 7) — [winit canvas.rs](https://github.com/rust-windowing/winit/blob/v0.30.x/src/platform_impl/web/web_sys/canvas.rs)
- **Release context:** Bevy 0.18 was released 2026-01-13 — [Bevy 0.18 release post](https://bevy.org/news/bevy-0-18/)

### Inferences
- Repository fact (not a web source): `crates/client/Cargo.toml` pins `bevy = "0.18"`, `winit = "0.30"`, `wgpu = "27"`; the native build enables `multi_threaded`, the wasm build does not. Pipelined rendering therefore applies to the desktop build only.
- Native worst case with defaults (pipelined rendering + vsync FIFO + frame latency 2): input read at frame start, one frame to simulate, one frame in the render thread, up to two queued frames, then scan-out: about 3 to 5 frames, i.e. 50 to 83 ms at 60 Hz or 21 to 35 ms at 144 Hz, before display lag. The likely low-latency native configuration is `AutoNoVsync` (or Mailbox), `desired_maximum_frame_latency = 1`, a frame limiter/pacer just under refresh, and evaluating whether pipelined rendering's extra frame is worth its throughput. These are candidates to measure, not published results; the Bevy maintainers themselves asked for benchmarks.
- Desktop and browser builds will differ in feel for structural reasons (present mode, raw input, pipelining), so a "feels off" report should always be tagged with which build and which browser.
- If the client applies view angles inside the fixed step or renders the first-person camera from an interpolated transform, Bevy's own example describes that as the thing not to do; this is the first place to look for "heavy" aim (the code audit belongs to another researcher).

### Gaps
- I could not query Bevy's issue tracker directly (GitHub API not available in this session); the issues cited are those surfaced by search, some through mirror URLs.
- Bevy 0.19/0.20 release notes were not read; any latency-relevant changes after 0.18 are unknown to these notes.
- No published end-to-end latency measurements of a Bevy app (native or wasm) were found.
- Whether Bevy/winit plan to pass `unadjustedMovement` on the web was not found.

## 11. Known failure modes of this architecture, with the symptom as a player would describe it

### Takeaway
Most "combat feels off" reports in server-authoritative shooters map to a small set of timing faults, each documented by at least one studio; the table below pairs the player's words with the mechanism and the published evidence so each can be tested separately.

### Cited Findings
- **"I died behind cover" / "shot around the corner":** favor-the-shooter rewind plus the victim's own latency and interpolation; grows with either player's ping and with movement speed; unbounded without a rewind cap — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience); [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode); [Gambetta](https://www.gabrielgambetta.com/lag-compensation.html)
- **"My shots don't register" / "blood but no damage":** hit feedback predicted on the client that the server does not confirm; local simulation drifting under loss; non-point bullets; dying on the server before the client knows so later shots are discarded; bandwidth prioritisation dropping the relevant update — [Respawn/EA deep dive](https://www.ea.com/games/apex-legends/news/servers-netcode-developer-deep-dive); [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience); [BLAST.tv on CS2 damage prediction](https://blast.tv/cs/news/damage-prediction-in-cs2)
- **"He was already moving when he shot me accurately" / "the kill feed and what I saw disagree":** interpolation delay desynchronises movement data from damage data (7.8 ms even in Valorant); corpses blocking view on the death frame — [Riot, 04 post](https://playvalorant.com/en-gb/news/game-updates/04-on-peeker-s-advantage-ranked/)
- **"They saw me first" / "peekers always win":** peeker's advantage = enemy frame time + both one-way lags + server tick + interp delay; 40 to 70 ms in Valorant, more at lower tick or with larger buffers; players feel 10 ms of it — [Riot, 04 post](https://playvalorant.com/en-gb/news/game-updates/04-on-peeker-s-advantage-ranked/); [Riot tech blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/peeking-valorants-netcode)
- **"Enemies pop in out of nowhere when I peek":** server-side fog of war revealing too late (centre-ray tests, look-ahead smaller than ping); or revealed with stale event state (wrong pose) — [Riot Fog of War blog](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/demolishing-wallhacks-valorants-fog-war)
- **"Enemies are jittery / teleporty":** interpolation buffer too short for the loss and jitter present, or extrapolation of unpredictable motion; a buffer should survive two lost packets — [Gaffer On Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/snapshot_interpolation/); [Riot developer post](https://devtrackers.gg/valorant/p/e1133afd-peekers-advantage-strafe-shooting)
- **"Melee whiffs" / "we both got the back-smack" / "he ran through me":** two bodies' positions diverging between machines during lunges; each client's view validated separately — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience)
- **"I keep getting pulled back" / rubber-banding:** client and server simulations disagreeing (non-determinism), the server running out of the client's inputs, or the server missing its tick budget — [343, Closer Look](https://www.halowaypoint.com/news/closer-look-halo-infinite-online-experience); [Edgegap on Ford's talk](https://edgegap.com/blog/game-backend-deep-dive-overwatch-2016-netcode-architecture-rollback); [Respawn/EA deep dive](https://www.ea.com/games/apex-legends/news/servers-netcode-developer-deep-dive)
- **"Aim feels heavy / floaty / like vsync is on":** local latency: a camera updated at simulation rate or drawn from interpolated state, queued frames, compositor frames; about twice as damaging per ms as network latency — [Bevy fixed-timestep example](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/blob/main/examples/movement/physics_in_fixed_timestep.rs); [Bevy window docs](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/blob/v0.18.0/crates/bevy_window/src/window.rs); [Liu, Claypool et al.](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~claypool/papers/csgo-net-local-21/paper.pdf); [Nvidia](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/improving-player-performance-with-low-latency-as-evident-from-fps-aim-trainer-experiments/)
- **"My sensitivity is different in the browser" / "the view suddenly spins":** pointer lock without `unadjustedMovement` (OS acceleration applied), high-polling-rate delta spikes, and browser-specific pointer-lock bugs — [web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/disable-mouse-acceleration); [Stack Overflow thread (mirror)](https://kiwix.lema.org/stackoverflow.com_en_all_2023-11/questions/47985847/javascript-mouseevent-movementx-and-movementy-large-spikes); [Mozilla bug 2055058](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=2055058)
- **"Movement and shooting feel a beat late since the update" (even with correct hit results):** precise server-side timing with visuals still tied to the next tick, as players reported of CS2's sub-tick — [Sportskeeda](https://sportskeeda.com/esports/even-64-tick-better-cs2-community-debates-sub-tick-system)
- **"Everything went slow-motion / the whole lobby lagged":** the server exceeding its tick budget — [Respawn/EA deep dive](https://www.ea.com/games/apex-legends/news/servers-netcode-developer-deep-dive)

### Inferences
- Additional failure modes that follow from the cited mechanisms but for which I have no direct published account:
  - "My gun fires late for other people" / "hit markers come late": server input buffer depth plus tick wait plus return trip; worst for clients that render slowly and send input in bursts (browser tabs under load).
  - "Gunshots and hit sounds lag the picture" in the browser: audio output latency of 10 to 40 ms on desktop and more on Linux, Android and Bluetooth.
  - "Enemies slide for a moment when they come into view": a newly revealed entity with no interpolation history.
  - "Hitch, then everyone warps": a TCP fallback transport under packet loss, or a main-thread stall in single-threaded wasm (shader compile, asset decode).
  - "Big Team feels worse than 2v2": a lower tick or snapshot rate in the large mode (as in Halo Infinite's 30 vs 60 Hz) lengthening every term in the peeker and shot-delay sums, with vehicles multiplying the position error.
  - "Shields pop again after I'm safe": late lag-compensated damage arriving after cover is reached, which also interferes with recharge timing.
- A practical test order implied by the evidence, cheapest and highest-impact first: (1) local path on each build: where view angles are applied, camera interpolation, present mode and queue depth, raw mouse input in the browser; (2) the rewind time the server uses versus the interpolation time the client actually rendered; (3) interpolation buffer length versus real snapshot loss and jitter; (4) fog-of-war reveal margin per client latency and reveal-time state; (5) server input-buffer depth distribution per client type (desktop vs browser); (6) tick and send rate per mode.
- Bungie's method is worth copying as process: give playtesters a "that felt laggy" button and iterate until presses stop correlating with the events being tuned (cited in section 3, Wolfire's summary of Aldridge).

### Gaps
- No single published source catalogues these failure modes together; the mapping of symptoms to causes for the inferred rows is mine.
- I found no public post-mortem of a browser-based competitive shooter's netcode to confirm which of these dominate on the web in practice.
